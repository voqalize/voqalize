Ask a lighting designer what separates a room that feels finished from one that does not, and the answer is rarely the fixtures themselves. It is the number of them and the heights they sit at. A single overhead light pours down from one point, flattens faces, and leaves the corners in murk. Light coming from several heights, each a little softer, makes the same room look deeper, warmer and more considered.

Designers talk about three layers: ambient, task and accent. You do not need the vocabulary to use the idea. Think instead about three heights in the room, the ceiling, standing height and table height, and make sure each one has something glowing at it once the sun goes down.

## Start at the ceiling, then turn it down

The overhead light is the one you switch on when you walk in with your arms full of shopping. It needs to be bright enough for cleaning and searching for keys, and it should not be the light you live under all evening. The two things that make the biggest difference are the shade and a dimmer.

## Standing height: the forgotten middle

Most rooms miss this layer entirely, and it is the one that makes evenings feel generous. A floor lamp fills the space between the ceiling and the furniture, lifts dark corners and gives the walls a gentle wash of light. It is also the easiest way to light a reading chair without drilling into anything.

Place floor lamps where shadows gather: a corner behind a sofa, the far end of a long room, beside a chair that faces away from the window. Aim for the shade to sit just above eye level when you are seated so the bulb is never in view.

## Table height: where the warmth lives

Table lamps are the layer that people notice and remember. They sit near eye level when you are seated, so they shape how faces look and how a room feels in conversation. Put them on side tables, consoles and nightstands, and do not be afraid of having several in one room. Three table lamps in a living room is not too many.

Pairs bring order, single lamps bring character. A matching pair either side of a sofa or bed reads as calm and classic. A single lamp on a console, balanced by a plant or a stack of books on the other side, feels more relaxed.

## Bedrooms need low light most of all

The bedroom is where overhead light is least welcome. Once you are getting ready for sleep, you want light below eye level and as warm as possible. Bedside lamps should let you read without lighting up the whole room for someone already asleep, and their switches should be easy to find in the dark.

## A few rules that always help

- Choose warm bulbs for living spaces and bedrooms. Light that leans golden flatters wood, linen and skin; very cool light belongs in a garage.
- Match the warmth of every bulb in the room. Mixing warm and cool sources is the most common reason a room feels slightly off.
- Put the overhead light on a dimmer if you can, and use it at half strength more often than full.
- Switch lamps on before the room gets dark, not after. Lighting a room at dusk feels gentle; lighting it in darkness feels abrupt.
- Hide bare bulbs. If you can see the bulb from where you normally sit, the shade is too high or too open.

## Walk the room at night

The best test is the simplest. One evening, turn off the ceiling light and switch on everything else. Walk to each seat and look around. Is there a dark corner that pulls the eye? Is any lamp glaring into someone's face? Is there a spot where you would want to read but cannot see the page? Fix those three things, one lamp at a time, and you will have a room that looks its best exactly when you are home to enjoy it.
