There is a kind of dinner party that makes guests nervous: a long row of glasses at each place, three forks, a centrepiece so tall nobody can see across it. And there is the kind people remember for years, where the table looked beautiful but nobody worried about which glass was theirs. The second kind is easier to set, and it is what we want to help you with.

A table for friends should feel like an invitation to stay. That means it should be easy to reach across, easy to clear and lit low enough that everyone looks their best. Here is how to put one together.

## Start with a bare table

If your table has a beautiful surface, let it show. Wood that has been used for years, with its knots and marks, is warmer than any tablecloth. Wipe it down and, if you like, use a runner down the centre to protect it from hot dishes and to give the serving pieces a home.

If your table is less lovely, use a linen cloth in a natural tone, left slightly rumpled. A crisp, starched white cloth signals formality; a soft, washed linen one says "stay a while".

## Build each place from the bottom up

A relaxed place setting needs only four things: a plate, a napkin, cutlery and a glass. Add a bowl if you are serving soup or pasta, and a second glass if there will be both water and wine.

Lay the dinner plate first, a thumb's width in from the table edge. Forks go to the left, knives to the right with the blade facing the plate, spoons to the right of the knife. Glasses sit above the knife. If there are courses, cutlery goes in the order it will be used, working from the outside in. That is genuinely the only rule worth remembering.

Leave enough space between places for elbows. As a rough guide, allow about sixty centimetres per person, and a little more if there is room. A crowded table feels festive for the first ten minutes and cramped for the next two hours.

Mismatched plates are perfectly fine, as long as they share something: a colour family, a material or a shape. Variety within a theme looks collected. Variety without one looks like the dishwasher was half full.

## Napkins: generous and natural

Cloth napkins are the single cheapest upgrade to any table. Choose a natural fibre, fold loosely, and lay them on the plate, to the left of the forks, or tucked under the cutlery. Avoid elaborate folds; they look as though they took effort, and they did.

## Serve from the centre

Family-style serving, with dishes passed around, is friendlier than plating in the kitchen. Guests help themselves and each other, conversation flows around the passing, and the host gets to sit down. Put serving dishes on boards or trivets down the middle, and leave a gap at each place so they can be set down within reach.

## Keep the centrepiece low

The rule is simple: you should be able to see the person opposite you over it. Anything taller than a seated person's chin belongs on the sideboard. Low, loose arrangements are best, in a few small vessels spaced down the table rather than one large one in the middle.

## Light with candles, not the ceiling

Turn the overhead light down or off, and let candles do the work. Candlelight is warm, low and flattering. Group candles at different heights, and use unscented ones at the table so they do not compete with the food.

## A drink at the door

Start the evening before anyone sits down. Have something ready to pour as guests arrive, whether that is a cocktail, a glass of something sparkling or a jug of something cold without alcohol. It sets the tone and gives you a few minutes to finish in the kitchen.

## The last five minutes

Before guests arrive, sit in each chair. Can you see everyone? Can you reach the water? Is any candle in someone's eyeline? Fix what you notice, light the candles, and pour yourself a drink. The table is ready when you are relaxed enough to enjoy it, and that, more than any arrangement of forks, is what your guests will remember.
