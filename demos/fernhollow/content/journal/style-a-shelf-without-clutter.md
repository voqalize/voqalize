Open shelves are one of the great pleasures of a home and one of its most common frustrations. In theory they are a place to show what you love. In practice they fill up with whatever was in your hands when you walked past: a stray candle, last month's post, a souvenir you are not sure about. Within a year the shelf that was meant to be a display has become a drawer without a front.

Styling a shelf well is not about buying more objects. It is about editing, grouping and giving each thing enough space that it can be seen. Here is the method stylists use, step by step.

## Empty it completely

Start with a bare shelf. Take everything off, wipe it down and look at it empty for a minute. You will notice its proportions, the colour of the wood and the wall behind it, and where the light falls. That is the stage you are setting.

Then sort what came off into three piles: things you love, things you need nearby, and things that ended up there by accident. The third pile leaves the room. The second pile probably belongs in a box or basket. Only the first pile goes back on display, and even that will be edited further.

## Start with the anchors

Place your largest items first. On a bookshelf that usually means books; on a display shelf it might be a vase, a framed print or a lamp. Spread these anchors across the shelves in a loose zigzag rather than stacking all the big things on one level. Your eye should travel diagonally from top left to bottom right, or the reverse, without getting stuck.

Books work hardest when you vary them. Stand some upright, lean them against a side, and lay a short stack of three or four horizontally. A horizontal stack makes a natural plinth for a small object on top.

## Group in odd numbers

Once the anchors are down, gather the smaller objects into groups of three or five. Odd numbers look natural; pairs and fours look arranged. Within each group, vary height and shape: something tall and slender, something low and wide, something round. The classic trio is a tall vase, a medium object and a small, low piece in front.

Overlap a little. Placing a small object slightly in front of a larger one creates depth. Lining everything up along the front edge flattens it.

## Leave a third empty

This is the rule people resist most, and the one that matters most. Empty space is not wasted space; it is what lets each group read as a group. As a rough guide, aim to leave about a third of each shelf clear. If a shelf feels finished, take one thing away and look again.

## Limit the palette

A shelf looks calm when the colours are few. Choose two or three tones and stick to them, perhaps the colour of the wood, a cream or white, and one accent such as terracotta or a deep green. Books with bright spines can be turned around, grouped by colour, or covered, depending on how much you need to find them. Repeat each colour at least twice across the shelves so it looks intentional.

Mix materials, though. Ceramic beside wood beside glass beside linen gives the eye texture without adding colour.

## Hide the necessary

Shelves often have to work as storage too. Give the practical things a home that looks considered. Put the lowest shelf to work with baskets or boxes, which hide clutter and visually anchor the unit from below. Keep the most decorative objects at eye level, where they will be seen.

## Live with it, then adjust

When you think you are done, walk away. Come back the next morning and look at the shelf from the doorway and from the sofa, the places you actually see it from. Something will stand out as wrong: a group too crowded, a gap that feels like a missing tooth. Move one thing at a time.

And keep the shelf honest. Once a week, take off anything that has drifted onto it without an invitation. A well-styled shelf is not a one-off project; it is a small habit of returning things to where they belong, and that habit is what keeps the calm.
