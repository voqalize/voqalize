Small living rooms tend to get furnished with good intentions and bad results. Worried about space, people buy small pieces, and lots of them: a little sofa, a little armchair, two little side tables, a little rug floating in the middle. The result is a room that feels crowded and somehow still empty, with nothing substantial enough to anchor it.

The rooms that feel generous do something different. They use fewer pieces, choose them for their proportions rather than just their size, and give the eye clear lines and open floor. Here is how.

## Measure the paths before the furniture

The first thing a small room needs is a clear way through. Mark the routes people actually walk: from the door to the sofa, from the sofa to the window, from the room to the kitchen. Keep those paths open. As a general guide, a main walkway is comfortable at around ninety centimetres wide, and the gap between a sofa and a coffee table is comfortable at a little under half a metre.

Once the paths are clear, everything else fits around them. If a piece blocks a path, it is either the wrong piece or in the wrong place.

## Choose a sofa that is light on its feet

The sofa is the largest thing in the room, so its shape matters more than anything else. In a small space, look for pieces with visible legs, a slim profile and tailored lines. When you can see floor under a sofa, the room reads as larger, because the eye measures space by how much floor it can see.

Push the sofa against the longest wall only if that is where it works best. Sometimes floating it a little away from the wall, even by a hand's width, makes a room feel more relaxed and less like a waiting room.

## Round edges and raised legs

Coffee tables in small rooms should be easy to walk around. Round or rounded tables are kinder to shins and keep the flow moving. Tables raised on legs, rather than solid blocks to the floor, leave the floor visible.

## Go low and long with storage

Tall, deep storage units loom over a small room. Low pieces keep the top half of the walls clear, which makes the ceiling feel higher. A long, low console under the television can hold everything a living room needs to hide while doubling as a surface for a lamp and a few objects.

## Use mirrors to borrow light

A mirror placed well does two jobs: it bounces daylight deeper into the room and it creates a sense of depth. The best position is on a wall adjacent to or opposite a window, where it can catch and reflect the view. Avoid hanging one where it simply reflects a blank wall or a cluttered corner.

## Light from several corners

Small rooms often rely on one ceiling light, which throws shadows into the corners and makes the room's edges disappear. Light the corners instead. A floor lamp in one corner and a table lamp on the console make the walls glow, and glowing walls feel farther away.

## Size the rug up, not down

This is the most common mistake in small rooms. A small rug floating in the middle of the floor chops the room into pieces and makes it look smaller. A larger rug, big enough for at least the front legs of the sofa and chairs to sit on, unifies the seating area into a single, generous zone.

## Keep the windows light

Heavy curtains swallow small rooms. Choose light, airy fabric in a pale colour and hang it high, close to the ceiling, with the rod extending past the window on each side. When open, the curtains should sit almost entirely beside the glass, not in front of it. The window will look bigger and the room will get every bit of daylight available.

## Edit, then edit again

The final step is the hardest: take something out. Most small rooms improve with one piece fewer than you think they need. A side table you rarely use, an extra chair that only matters at Christmas, a stack of baskets. Remove one item for a week and see if you miss it. Usually, you will only notice that the room feels larger, and that is the whole idea.
