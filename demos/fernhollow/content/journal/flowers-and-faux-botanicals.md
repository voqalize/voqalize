Fresh flowers are one of life's simple luxuries, and nothing quite replaces the scent of a garden rose or the way tulips lean towards the window over a week. But fresh flowers ask for things: water, sunlight, a weekly budget, and someone at home to look after them. For the corners of a home where those things are hard to provide, a dark hallway, a high shelf, a guest room used once a month, good faux botanicals have earned their place.

The trouble is that many faux arrangements give themselves away from across the room. They are too perfect, too symmetrical and too bright. The good news is that the same principles that make fresh flowers look natural apply to faux ones. Here is how to get it right.

## Choose varieties that are believable

Some plants are much easier to imitate than others. Leaves with a matte surface, like eucalyptus, olive and many ferns, translate beautifully. Flowers with structured, waxy petals, like magnolia, also look convincing. Delicate, translucent flowers are harder to fake well. As a rule, if you would rarely see the real thing in a vase in someone's home, a faux version will look out of place.

## Fewer stems, more air

Real bouquets are rarely packed tight. Stems lean, cross and leave gaps. The single most effective thing you can do with a faux arrangement is take some stems out and spread the rest. Give each one room, let some sit higher than others, and allow at least one to break the outline.

A single stem in a small vase is often the most convincing of all, because it invites a close look and holds up to it.

## Bend everything

Straight stems are a giveaway. Most faux stems have a wire core, which means you can shape them. Before arranging, curve each stem slightly, tilt the flower heads in different directions, and open the leaves so they are not all facing forward. Look at a real plant and notice how no two leaves sit at the same angle. Copy that.

## The vessel matters as much as the flowers

A faux arrangement in a cheap plastic pot reads as fake no matter how good the stems are. A real material, ceramic, stone, glass or wood, lends its credibility to whatever is inside. Choose matte finishes and natural colours over glossy brights.

If you use a clear glass vase, consider adding a little water. It is an old stylist's trick: real water in the glass makes the eye believe the stems are real too. Change it now and then so it stays clear.

## Put them where real plants would struggle

Faux botanicals are most useful exactly where real ones would fail: rooms with little natural light, high shelves that are awkward to water, or places where a plant would be easily knocked. A potted arrangement on top of a bookcase or in a windowless bathroom brings life to a spot that would otherwise stay bare.

Avoid placing faux flowers right next to fresh ones of the same type. The comparison does them no favours. Let each have its own part of the room.

## Neutral palettes are more forgiving

Very bright colours are the hardest to get right in faux flowers; slightly off shades are easy to spot. Creams, whites, soft greens and muted earth tones are much more forgiving and suit a calm room better anyway.

## Scale for the space

A large room or an entrance hall can carry a substantial arrangement, but it should still look loose and natural. Make it the only arrangement in the space and give it a surface of its own, without other objects crowding it.

## Keep them looking fresh

Faux flowers collect dust, and dust is what finally gives them away. Every couple of weeks, blow them gently with a hairdryer on its cool setting or wipe leaves with a soft, dry cloth. Rearrange them each season, too: change the vase, move them to a new room, swap a few stems. Real flowers change over the week, and a little change keeps faux ones from becoming part of the furniture.

And whenever you can, bring in something real as well. A branch from the garden, a handful of herbs, a single stem from the market. The mixture of real and faux, each in its place, is what makes a home feel alive.
