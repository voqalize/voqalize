Most home offices begin as an apology. A laptop on the kitchen table, a dining chair borrowed for "just this week", a lamp dragged in from the bedroom. Months later the arrangement is still there, and so is the sore neck. The good news is that a room you look forward to sitting down in is less about square footage than about a handful of decisions made in the right order.

That order matters. People tend to shop for the desk first because it is the biggest piece, then try to make everything else fit around it. Work the other way. Start with where you will sit and what you will look at, then light, then storage, and leave the objects that make it yours for last.

## Find the right wall before the right desk

Walk the room at the time of day you work hardest. Notice where daylight falls and where it glares. The ideal spot puts a window to your side rather than directly behind your screen (which turns you into a silhouette on video calls) or directly in front of it (which makes you squint all afternoon). Side light is also the most flattering light there is, which you will appreciate every time the camera turns on.

If you can, face into the room rather than into a corner. A view across the space, even a short one, gives your eyes somewhere to rest between tasks. When the only option is a wall, make that wall worth looking at: a single framed print or a shelf at eye level does more than a blank plane of paint.

## Choose a desk that can change its mind

Work changes. A desk that suited a laptop and a notebook may need to hold a second monitor next year, or a sewing machine, or a child's homework at the far end. That is why we lean towards pieces that can be reconfigured rather than replaced.

Whatever you choose, sit at it before you commit to its place. Your elbows should fall roughly level with the work surface when your shoulders are relaxed. If they do not, the fix is usually the chair, not the desk.

## The chair is the real investment

You will touch your chair more than any other piece of furniture in the house, bed aside. Spend accordingly. Look for adjustment you will actually use: height, tilt, and some support in the small of your back. Sit for a full ten minutes when you try one, because the first thirty seconds tell you almost nothing.

## Light in three layers, even in a small room

Overhead light alone flattens a workspace and tires the eyes. Plan for three sources instead. Daylight is the first. The second is a task light on the desk, placed on the side opposite your writing hand so your own arm never throws a shadow across the page. The third is a soft ambient light somewhere else in the room, so that on dark winter afternoons the screen is not the brightest thing you can see.

## Storage that keeps the surface clear

A clear desk is not about virtue; it is about attention. Everything visible in your peripheral vision is a small request for thought. The aim is to keep only today's work within reach and give everything else a home you can get to in a few seconds.

Wall shelving is the most useful storage in a small office because it uses space you were not using anyway. Mount the lowest shelf high enough to clear your monitor and your head when you stand up, and keep the heaviest things, such as reference books, closest to the brackets.

## Finish with a few things you love

Once the room works, let it say something about you. Limit the desk itself to three objects that are not tools: perhaps a plant, a photograph and something beautiful that tells the time. Anything more and you are back to clutter.

## A short checklist

Before you call it finished, sit down at the end of a real working day and check:

- Light comes from the side, and nothing glares on the screen.
- Your feet are flat, your elbows level with the desk, your back supported.
- The desk holds only what you are working on today.
- There is one thing within sight that makes you happy to be there.

Get those right and the room will do what a good office should: disappear while you work, and welcome you back the next morning.
