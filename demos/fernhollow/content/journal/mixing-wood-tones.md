There is a persistent belief that every wooden thing in a room ought to match. It comes from the days of the bedroom suite, when the wardrobe, chest and nightstands arrived together in one finish and stayed together for forty years. Try to follow that rule today and you discover two problems. First, it is nearly impossible: wood changes colour with light and age, and two oak pieces from different makers rarely sit side by side without one looking slightly off. Second, it is flat. A room where every surface is the same brown reads like a showroom, not a home.

Mixed woods, chosen with a little intention, do the opposite. They give a room depth and the sense that it was collected over time. The trick is knowing what makes a mixture look deliberate rather than accidental.

## Know the three characters

It helps to think of the common furniture woods as personalities.

**Oak** is the steady middle. It runs from pale honey to a warm golden tone, with a visible, open grain that gives it texture even from across the room. Oak goes with almost everything, which is why it so often ends up as the backbone of a scheme.

**Walnut** is the deep voice. Rich chocolate browns, sometimes with purple or grey undertones, and a smoother, more flowing grain. It brings weight and a certain seriousness, so a little goes a long way.

**Ash** is the light touch. Pale, often close to cream, with a straight, lively grain. It keeps a room airy and pairs naturally with white walls and linen.

## Pick a lead wood, then add contrast

The most reliable approach is to choose one dominant wood, usually for the largest pieces, and introduce one or two others in smaller doses. A rough guide many designers use is sixty, thirty, ten: most of the wood in one tone, a good share in a second, and a final accent in a third.

The lead does not have to be the lightest. In a living room where the sofa has a walnut base, walnut might set the tone, with oak in the middle and ash for the smallest touches.

## Contrast clearly, not slightly

The mixtures that look wrong are almost always the ones that are close but not the same. A golden oak beside a slightly redder oak looks like a mistake. A golden oak beside a deep walnut looks like a choice. When you add a second wood, make it obviously different in depth: light against dark, or dark against light.

## Repeat each wood at least twice

A single piece in a wood that appears nowhere else can feel like an orphan. The cure is repetition. If you bring walnut into the room through a sofa base, echo it somewhere else, ideally at a different height, so the eye travels.

## Use the small things as bridges

Accessories are where the third wood earns its place. A candle holder, a tray or a picture frame in the accent tone links pieces that would otherwise sit apart. Put an ash candle holder on a walnut shelf and an oak one on the ash table, and suddenly the three woods are in conversation.

## Mind the undertones

Every wood leans warm or cool. Most oaks and walnuts lean warm, towards red and yellow; some walnuts and grey-washed finishes lean cool. You can mix undertones, but a room feels most settled when the majority share one. If your floor is a warm oak, warm woods on top will feel at home.

Your floor counts as one of your woods. It is the largest wooden surface in the room, so treat it as the lead, or put a rug between it and the furniture so the two do not compete directly.

## Let textiles do the mediating

When woods start to feel busy, fabric is the peacemaker. A rug under the coffee table separates its wood from the floor's. Linen cushions, a wool throw or curtains in a neutral colour give the eye a rest between wooden surfaces. Pale, natural textiles flatter every wood tone, which is why they appear in so many rooms that mix woods well.

## A quick test

Stand at the door and squint. Wood tones will merge into broad areas of light, middle and dark. If you can see all three spread across the room rather than clustered on one side, you have it. If one corner is all dark and another all pale, move a lamp or a shelf of objects across the room until the balance comes back. Mixing woods is less a rule than a habit of looking, and the more you do it, the more natural it feels.
